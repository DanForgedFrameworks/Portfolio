The scope as I understand it

From the advert and your message

The work

  • Review nine existing SCORM courses. The source files aren't available, so the content comes from the published versions.
  • Rebuild each course in Rise 360, with a better layout and learning experience. This is mostly a rebuild, not a redesign.
  • Make each course accessible to WCAG 2.2 AA, as far as Rise allows.
  • Publish and test all nine as SCORM on the client's LMS.
  • Hand over nine editable Rise projects and their assets, so the client can maintain them.

Provided by the client

  • The existing SCORM courses
  • A Rise licence for the project
  • An LMS test environment
  • Subject matter experts for content questions

How I'd run it

Eight steps, with an early check before the bulk of the build
  1. Set-up with the stakeholder. We agree the branding kit and the look and feel. This can take a day on its own, assuming all nine courses share one look.
  2. Unpack and audit. I unwrap each package, list its lessons and media, and mark what I can reuse.
  3. Front-load the SME questions. I send every content query early, so SME turnaround doesn't hold up the build.
  4. Early review. I build the first lesson, or a day's work. Then I review it with the stakeholder and the SME. We check the branding and the build line up. Then we crack on, without going too far down the road first.
  5. Build the rest in Rise, course by course.
  6. Accessibility QA on every course. The next section says how.
  7. Publish and test. I publish each course as SCORM and test it on the client's LMS.
  8. Final review and handover. I make the last fixes, then hand over the nine Rise projects and their assets.

Accessibility

Testing and fixes, done by me
  • I do the accessibility testing and the fixes myself. WCAG 2.2 AA and EN 301 549 are part of how I work, not an add-on.
  • Every course goes through an agentic QA pipeline built from checks I already use, plus an automated checker. A person signs off every stage.
  • I measure contrast rather than judge it by eye. Every course also gets a keyboard-only pass.
  • Rise has limits. Anything Rise can't fix, I record and report back to you.

My accessibility statement sets out how I check every build: Accessibility and inclusion statement.

The estimate

Days per course, and two worked examples

These figures come from my experience with similar clients. They're provisional until I've seen the packages.

The rules of thumb

  • Time available: six weeks at four days a week is 24 working days.
  • Set-up: up to 1 day, once, for the branding kit and the look and feel.
  • A compact one-lesson course: up to 3 days. That covers the audit, rebuild, accessibility, review and SCORM testing.
  • Longer courses: about 1.5 days a lesson to build. Review doesn't grow lesson for lesson: about half a day for one lesson, and closer to three-quarters of a day for three.

What can push it up

  • A like-for-like rebuild is the quickest case. An accessibility upgrade and refresh can take as long as a new build.
  • Complex subjects with several layers and lots of assets take longer than compact, straightforward ones.
  • With no source files, some media may need rebuilding. Images and links will need checking and refreshing.
  • If there's no planned audience or learning personas yet, I'd add that to the set-up work.
  • Storyline interactions take longer. The next section suggests an alternative.

Storyline

Why I'd suggest HTML inside Rise instead

I'd move away from Storyline unless the client insists on it. Storyline adds layers and build time, and on this timescale that matters.

I build the same interactions, and often more, as HTML packages that run inside Rise. They stay editable, and they're built to the same accessibility standard. A few examples, each running inside a Rise course:

  • Confidence self-check
  • Reveal cards
  • Chart-choice swipe
  • Magnifier
  • Reflection notepad
  • 3D molecule viewer with a case study

What I'd need for a firm estimate

Six things to see or know
  • How many lessons each course has, and how long each lesson is.
  • How complex each original SCORM is, and what media is inside it.
  • The planned audience and any learning personas.
  • How available the SMEs are, and how quickly they can reply.
  • Which SCORM version the client's LMS needs, 2004 or 1.2. It's a minor point, but it's good to know.
  • Where the courses came from: the client's own content, or bought in from someone else.
